MATN:
Scattered across the abyssal plains of the Pacific, four kilometres down, lie trillions of potato-sized lumps rich in nickel, cobalt and manganese. These nodules, which grow by a few millimetres every million years, have attracted intense interest because the metals they contain are needed for the batteries of electric vehicles. Proponents argue that gathering them would spare rainforests from open-cast mining and reduce dependence on a handful of supplier countries. Marine biologists are uneasy. The nodules are the only hard surface on an otherwise muddy seabed, and a host of creatures — many still unnamed — live attached to them. Test sites disturbed in the 1980s show scant signs of recovery four decades on. The dilemma is uncomfortable: the technologies intended to protect one environment may be purchased at the expense of another about which we know almost nothing.
